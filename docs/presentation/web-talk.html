Agent, model, harness — how they fit

An agent is a model plus a harness. That is the whole definition.

Without a harness you have a model doing one-off calls: you ask, it answers, it forgets. Useful, but transactional. The harness is what turns that into something that can actually do a job.

So "building an agent" is mostly not about the model. It is about what you put around it — which is the rest of this talk.

When it goes wrong in public

Four incidents that were widely reported. Each one is a missing pattern.

The one-dollar car

A dealership's support chatbot was told to agree with whatever the customer said — and then "agreed" to sell a vehicle for a dollar. Screenshots went round the internet the same day.

Missing: check what comes in. The instruction arrived in the message and nothing read it first.

The refund policy that did not exist

An airline's chatbot described a bereavement refund the airline did not offer. A tribunal later held the airline to what its bot had said.

Missing: grounding, and a human gate. It answered from nowhere, and nobody checked before it committed the company.

Source code into a public chatbot

Engineers reportedly pasted internal code into a public assistant to get help with it. The code left the building in the process.

Missing: a boundary. Nothing decided what was allowed to leave before it left.

Knight Capital, 2012

Not AI at all — automated trading ran unchecked after a bad deployment and lost around 440 million dollars in roughly 45 minutes.

Missing: a bound, and a person. The oldest lesson here, and the most expensive.

Three of these were not clever attacks. They were systems doing exactly what they were told, quickly, with nobody in the way.

Speaker's note

The slide that makes the stakes real. Keep the tone factual rather than gleeful — these are publicly reported incidents, and the point is the missing control, not the embarrassment. The dealership one is the cleanest illustration of prompt injection you will find: the customer simply told the bot to agree with everything, and it did, because nothing read the message before the model did. The airline one is the important one for anyone building for a business, because it establishes the thing executives care about: your agent's words can bind you. Knight Capital is deliberately not an AI story — it is there to show the pattern predates all of this, and that automation at speed with no bound and no human is a known way to lose a company in under an hour. If asked for numbers on the others, be honest that the reporting varies and the lesson does not depend on the figure.

The loop, as a picture

The model cannot do anything. It asks, you do it, and it looks at what came back.

Round and round until it has enough. One lap is a turn. There are only two ways out — it stops asking, which is the answer, or it runs out of laps, which is not. Telling those two apart is your job, not the model's.

Speaker's note

The picture the whole talk hangs on, so take it slowly and point as you go. The model does not execute anything — it emits a request. Your code runs the tool and hands the result back. The model looks at that and decides whether it needs more. One lap is a turn. Then the two exits, and the difference matters enormously: leaving because it has nothing left to ask is success, leaving because it hit the cap or threw is a run that was cut off with some text attached. From outside they look identical unless you make them look different. Everything else in this talk lives on one of these arrows — the check on the way in, the box around the tool, the save after each lap, the person who says yes before the risky one.

The loop everyone writes first

This is a working agent. It is also unshippable, and every reason why is one of the seven.

while True:
    reply = model(messages)
    if not reply.tool_calls:
        return reply.text
    for call in reply.tool_calls:
        out = TOOLS[call.name](**call.args)
        messages.append(out)
  • Nothing makes it stop. No bound on turns, none on spend.
  • One failure loses the lot. Nothing saved, nothing to resume. → 2 · 6
  • It can spend your money without asking. → 7
  • Whatever the model says, runs. No check in, no box round the tool. → 1 · 3
  • You cannot tell if a change helped. Nothing measures it.

Seven lines of code; seven patterns to make them safe. The last problem this repo only solves for the safety layer — there is no general eval harness here.

Speaker's note

The motivation slide, and the one that makes the seven feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. Read the code out — it genuinely works, and almost everyone in the room has written it. Then walk the failures in order, and let the pattern numbers land as you go: nothing stops it; one failure loses the lot, which is durable execution and supervision; it can spend money with nobody asked, which is the human gate; whatever the model emits gets executed, which is guardrails and the sandbox. Save the last one for the end because it is the least obvious and the most expensive to retrofit — without evaluations you cannot tell whether any change you make is an improvement, so you end up tuning prompts on vibes. How the loop ends

Two ways out, and they are not the same thing. Only one of them is success.

the good exit

It asked for no tools

The model came back with words and no tool calls. It is answering rather than working, so there is nothing left to do. That is the loop's exit condition — and the only clean one.

the other exit

It ran out

It hit the step cap, or something threw. The loop stops, but the work is unfinished. Do not dress this up as a result — a run that was cut off is a failure with a partial answer attached.

the trap

Wait for the whole reply

While it streams you only have part of the message. Append it early and the next turn reads a half-finished sentence as gospel. Let the stream finish, then push it.

Both exits leave the loop. Only one of them means the job is done — and telling them apart in your own code is what stops a truncated run being reported as a success.

Speaker's note

Three things that all live at the bottom of the loop. First, the exit condition: no tool calls this turn means the model is answering rather than working, and that is the only clean way out. Second, the other way out — hitting the step cap or throwing an error — which people casually call getting kicked out of the loop. It is worth being pedantic here, because the two look identical from outside if you are careless: both stop the loop, both leave you holding some text, and only one of them means the task is finished. If your code does not distinguish them, you will eventually ship a truncated run to a user as though it were an answer. Third, the streaming trap: you can only append a COMPLETE assistant message, and a half-streamed reply pushed into the history becomes something the model treats as settled fact on the next turn.

What a tool is made of

Whatever the language, every tool you hand an agent is the same three things.

1 · for the model

A description

What this tool does, and when to reach for it. The model reads it to decide. Write it for the model, not for the next developer.

2 · the contract

An input schema

Which arguments it takes, of what shape, which are required. Anything that does not fit is rejected before your code sees it.

3 · yours

An execute function

The part that actually does the thing. Ordinary code — and the only one of the three that is not talking to the model.

Same trio everywhere; only the spelling changes — zod in TypeScript, pydantic in Python. This repo defines no tools of its own: its agent uses Azure's.

Speaker's note

The shape of a tool, and worth drawing because the next two quizzes both live here. One and two are prompt engineering wearing an API's clothes: the description decides IF the tool is chosen, the schema decides whether the call is well-formed. Three is the only part that is just software. Check yourself — how the code is kept apart

Three about the machinery underneath pattern 3.

What actually keeps it apart?

It runs as a separate program, not inside yours — its own interpreter, started so that it ignores your installed packages and your environment. Whatever you do not pass in, it does not have.

Why a whole process, and not just a locked-down namespace?

Because you can kill a process. Python cannot safely stop a thread or a running exec half-way — an infinite loop inside your own program is yours forever. Separate process is what makes the time limit real.

Why hand back ok=True or ok=False?

Because the thing reading it is a model. One unambiguous flag beats making it infer failure from the shape of an error string. Say plainly whether it worked, then give the output or the error.

Ours returns exactly that — SandboxResult(ok, stdout, stderr, exit_code, timed_out). And it is not a security boundary: it shares the filesystem and network, caps neither memory nor CPU, and the timeout kills only the child it started, so anything that child spawns outlives it. The real answer is a managed sandbox — Foundry Code Interpreter, or Container Apps dynamic sessions.

Speaker's note

Three cards on how isolation actually works here, and the middle one is the one worth the time because it is the Python-specific reason for the whole design. Python gives you no safe way to stop a thread or interrupt a running exec from outside, so a snippet with an infinite loop in it is now part of your process for good. How the harness talks to itself

Three pieces you will meet in any harness built for more than one machine.

the loop

Where the agent runs

The core of the harness: receive, think, act, repeat, until the task is done — a game loop, for an agent. Many codebases put it in a file called runtime.

emit

Announce, do not ask

Say this happened and carry on. No waiting for a reply. Anything that cares — a log, a screen, another agent — reacts in its own time. The event names live in one shared file, so both ends agree on them.

transport

Two-way, not one-way

Send a task and forget it; answers arrive later, and possibly from a different machine. That needs a two-way connection — WebSockets, not one-way server-sent events. And check the socket is actually open before you write to it; clients vanish without telling you.

Inside the event bus

Three small decisions that decide whether the record can be trusted.

order

Number every event

Each one gets a sequence number. One integer buys two things: replay in the order events really happened, and a way to notice the same event arriving twice.

order of operations

Write it down, then shout

Save the event first, tell the listeners second. Do it the other way round and a failed write leaves everyone believing something that never happened.

tidy up

Subscribing hands back an off switch

Subscribe returns a function that removes your listener. Call it when you are done. Skip it and listeners quietly pile up until something falls over.

And if the run is durable, the write belongs in a step too — an emit left floating in the workflow is not recorded, so a restart fires it again. This repo has no event bus: its API answers in one go, so there is nothing to number.

Speaker's note

Three decisions that look like plumbing and are not. Sequence numbers: events arrive late and sometimes twice, which is normal in anything distributed rather than a bug you can engineer away, so the number is how a replay reconstructs the true order and how a listener knows it has already handled forty-seven. Order of operations: persist before you notify. If you shout first and the write then fails, every client is now holding a fact your system has no record of — and that is far worse than a slightly slower event, because the disagreement is invisible. Subscribe returning its own cleanup is the one people skip: every listener you add and never remove is a leak, and in a long-lived process that is how you end up restarting the server every Tuesday. Our durable/ does the small version of the first idea — checkpoints keyed by run id plus step index, which is what lets a resume skip completed work — but there is no event log here to number.

Check yourself — the hard parts

Three that people most often get wrong.

What is durable execution?

Work that keeps going regardless of who is connected. It saves where it got to as it goes, so a crash, a restart or someone closing their laptop does not lose it — it picks up from the last save rather than the beginning. The run does not belong to the connection.

Why append every reply back onto the message list?

Because the model remembers nothing between calls — you resend the whole conversation every time. Leave out what it just said, or the result of a tool it just called, and the next turn has no record that any of it happened: it repeats itself, or loses the thread. The list is the memory.

What kinds of memory does a harness handle?

Four. Short-term — this conversation. Long-term — what it learned before. Scratch pad — working notes for one task, thrown away after. Facts — what the organisation knows, looked up rather than guessed. They have different lifetimes, so they need different storage.

Speaker's note

The two worth checking, because both are usually half-understood. On memory, the common wrong answer is 'the conversation history' — that is one of four, and conflating them is why so many agents either forget everything or carry an enormous prompt around. On durable execution, the common wrong answer is 'so it does not crash' — crashes are the easy half; the real driver is that a long job must outlive the user's session and still have progress to show. Be honest again here that this repo implements three of the four memories and has no scratch pad.

Check yourself — the loop, up close

Three that only bite once you try to make a real agent durable.

Why wrap the model call itself in a step?

Two reasons, and the second is the real one. It is slow and you already paid for it, so a resume should not buy it twice. But the model is also not deterministic — ask again and you may get a different answer, and the run takes a different path. Recording the result means a replay follows the route it took the first time. Cheaper is the bonus; faithful is the point.

How do you start a durable workflow?

Not by calling it. You hand the function to the engine, with its arguments, and the engine runs it — so it can record that it started, survive a restart, and be resumed later by id. Call it directly and you have an ordinary function that dies with the process. The exact spelling differs by library; the handover is the idea.

When do several operations belong in one step?

When they have to happen together or not at all. The clearest case: the event announcing a thing belongs in the same step as the thing. Split them and a crash in between leaves a client showing an action that never happened — or an action nobody was told about. A step is the unit that replays; draw it round what must stay consistent.

Speaker's note

Six that only matter once someone tries to build the real thing. On code mode: it is the difference between an agent that asks for one tool per turn and one that writes a short program calling several. The second is dramatically better — it can loop over results, filter, and combine, all without another round trip — and it is also the moment the safety question stops being theoretical. A menu of tools is a bounded set of things it can ask for. A program is not. That is the whole argument for pattern 3, and the reason a managed sandbox is worth paying for rather than a subprocess you wrote on a Friday. On grouping: the question is not how small you can make a step, it is what must stay consistent. A step replays as a unit, so anything that has to happen together belongs inside one — and the sharpest example is an emit paired with the operation it describes. Separate them and a crash in the gap gives you a client told about something that never happened, or a thing that happened with nobody told. Both are worse than redoing a little work. On starting a workflow: the syntax varies and does not matter — what matters is that you are not calling the function, you are giving it to something that will call it on your behalf. That is the whole difference between work that dies with your process and work that has an id, a record, and a way back. It is the same handover as wrapping an operation in a step, one level up, and the same rule underneath it: durability is only available to whoever is actually running the thing. On wrapping the model call: everyone reaches for the cost argument first, and it is true — a resumed run should not re-buy an expensive call it already made. But lead with determinism, because that is the one people have not thought about. The model is not a pure function. Re-run it during recovery and it can answer differently, pick a different tool, take a different branch, so your replay is no longer a replay — it is a second, divergent run wearing the same id. Recording the output is what makes recovery faithful; the saved money is a side effect. A turn is one pass through the loop — one model call, which either requests tools or answers. Tool call, then think again, is a new turn. It is the unit you cap with a step limit and the unit a resumed run counts from, so it is worth having a name for. Say the general form out loud, because it is the thread through the whole talk: you cannot make durable, or safe, or auditable, anything you are not the one executing. That is the same reason the model calling your tools directly would be a disaster, and it is why every pattern here lives on your side of that boundary.

Check yourself — the loop, continued

Two more, about control and cost.

What is “code mode”?

Instead of picking one tool at a time, the model writes a program that calls them — looping, filtering, chaining calls in one go instead of burning a turn on each. A job that took a dozen turns of ask-wait-think becomes one execution. It is also exactly why pattern 3 exists: the moment it is writing code rather than choosing from a menu, something has to run that code somewhere it cannot do harm.

What is a "turn"?

One trip round the loop: the model is called once, and either asks for tools or gives its answer. Ask for a tool, run it, call the model again — that is a second turn. Counting them is how you cap a runaway, and how a resumed run knows which trip it was on when the lights went out.

Speaker's note

The rest of the same set, and they share a theme worth naming: each is about who is in control. Code mode is the agent writing a short program instead of spending a turn per tool — dramatically better engineering, and the exact moment you stop being able to enumerate what might happen, which is why pattern three exists. A turn is one pass round the loop, the unit you cap with a step limit and the unit a resumed run counts from. Both land on the rule the whole talk keeps arriving at: you cannot make durable, or safe, or auditable, anything you are not the one running.

Check yourself — the fine print

Two about what you tell it, and what it costs you.

What is a system prompt, and what goes in it?

The standing instructions the builder gives the agent, separate from anything the user types: who it is, what it knows about the person it is talking to, how to behave, how to handle the work. They run to thousands of tokens and can eat a serious share of the context window — which is why the fact store lives outside it.

Why must a durable step be safe to run twice?

Because it will be. The save happens after the step, so a crash in that gap re-runs it — you get at least once, not exactly once. Fine for "work out the total". Not fine for "send the money". Make the action repeat-safe, usually with a key the other end deduplicates on, because no amount of cleverness in the checkpoint fixes it.

Speaker's note

Three closers. On streaming, be clear it is a user-experience answer rather than a speed one — the tokens arrive at the same rate either way, but a blank screen for twenty seconds reads as a crash, and streaming also lets someone kill a bad answer early instead of paying for all of it. This repo does not stream; say so. The system prompt one matters for cost as much as behaviour — thousands of tokens on every single turn, which is the same budget argument as the knowledge base, and worth linking back to memory. The send-reply one is the best argument for the human gate in the whole talk, so do not rush it: the agent that sends fifty unreviewed emails is not broken and has not been jailbroken. It was told to reply to the email, sending is how replying finishes, and it finished. That is why the gate cannot live in the prompt — you cannot instruct your way out of an agent doing exactly what you asked. Two about what reaches the person.

Why stream the text instead of waiting for all of it?

Because a long answer with nothing on screen looks broken. Streaming shows the words as they are produced, so the wait feels like progress rather than a hang — and the reader can stop it early when it is already going wrong. The total time is the same; the experience is not. This repo does not stream — its API answers in one go.

Why not just give an agent a "send reply" tool?

Because it will use it. Tell an agent its job is to reply to your email and it will send every draft it wrote, immediately, to finish the task — unreviewed, to real people, in your name. Drafting is safe; sending is not. That line is exactly where pattern 7 goes.
